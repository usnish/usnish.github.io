Same company, different state: who gets a dialysis chair after 5 pm

In-center hemodialysis takes about four hours, three times a week. For someone trying to keep a day job, the question is whether there is a chair after work, and how far away it is. The answer depends on the state, even within the same company.

What could change this

Medicare's public facility data record only whether a unit has a shift starting at 5 pm or later. The Late Shift field is a yes or no, and nothing in the CMS data dictionary records how many chairs that shift has or whether it is taking new patients. CMS could also ask units for both. By the yes/no measure, 21.8% of patients go to a unit with an evening shift; the data cannot say how many of those evening chairs are free.

One state already counts evening shifts. Since 2007, Washington's certificate-of-need rules for kidney dialysis centers have given applicants a point for a shift that begins after 5 pm (WSR 06-24-050; now WAC 246-310-827), and the current rule takes the answer from Medicare's Dialysis Facility Compare data or from a center's attestation. Washington has the highest share of the states and DC on this page, but this page cannot show that the rule is the reason.

About the data

Units and shifts. CMS Provider Data Catalog, Dialysis Facility – Listing by Facility (dataset 23ew-n7w9), . Every unit marked as offering in-center hemodialysis is counted. The CMS data dictionary defines its Late Shift field as “a shift starting at 5:00 P.M. or later”. Chains are the file's Chain Organization field; “Fresenius” is Fresenius Medical Care.

Patients. Patient shares weight each unit by the CMS column Number of adult HD patients with KT/V data, a count of adult hemodialysis patients the unit reported dialysis-adequacy lab results for over the reporting window. It is a stand-in for how many patients a unit treats, not a census on a given day (). Weighting by dialysis stations instead gives nationally, with the same ordering of states.

Distance. Units in the lower 48 states and DC were located from their street addresses with the Census Bureau geocoder (ZIP code center when the address did not match; ). For each of 2020 census tracts, the straight-line distance from its population center to the nearest unit, and to the nearest unit with a late shift, was weighted by the tract's population. Straight lines understate the road trip, and the nearest evening unit can be across a state line.

Change over time. The CMS archive snapshot of is compared with the current file, matching units on their CMS Certification Number. A unit that moved or was re-certified under a new number counts as one closure and one opening. The archive's 2021 and 2022 snapshots carry an identical copy of the 2020 facility file, so they are skipped.

What this cannot tell you. The late-shift field is a yes/no reported by each unit. It does not say how many chairs the evening shift has, whether it is full or open to new patients, or whether it is a 5 pm shift or an overnight (nocturnal) one. Where people live is not where patients live, and the nearest evening unit may be full, out of a patient's insurance network or too far by transit.

Prior work. The national share is not new: Crouch and colleagues reported that 16.0% of facilities offered late-shift services in 2020 CMS data, more often in urban than rural facilities (Am J Nephrol 2024); this page's 2020 snapshot gives of all facilities. Perez and colleagues linked evening shifts to employment at the facility level (CJASN 2025). What is new here is the patient-weighted share by state, the same-company comparison across states, the distance to an evening unit, and unit-level change since 2020. I could not find these published by KFF, its Health System Tracker, or the other policy and research sources I checked.
