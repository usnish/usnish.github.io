The dialysis time tax

By Usnish Majumdar, MD · Updated November 2026

Most people with kidney failure get hemodialysis at a center three times a week, every week, for years. This map estimates how much time the drive to the nearest dialysis center would take for the people living in every neighborhood in the country, and shows where households have no car to make that trip.

Vulnerable neighborhoods are not farther away, but far more of their households have no car

    The ranking leaves out counties where most residents have no drive time to a center (mostly Alaska). In the "No car and far away" ranking, LaGrange and Daviess counties in Indiana are home to two of the largest Amish settlements, where many households do not own cars by choice and hire drivers.

    Who pays for the ride

    Traditional Medicare pays for dialysis but, in most cases, not for the trip. It covers only an ambulance between home and the nearest dialysis center, and only when any other way of traveling could endanger the patient's health. For scheduled trips like these, the ambulance company needs a doctor's written order saying the ambulance is medically necessary. For repeated trips (3 or more round trips in 10 days, or at least weekly for 3 weeks), the patient or ambulance company can ask Medicare for prior authorization; if that is denied and the trips go on, Medicare denies the claims. (Medicare Coverage of Kidney Dialysis & Kidney Transplant Services, CMS, November 2025; Medicare.gov: ambulance services.)

    Medicaid must make sure enrollees who have no other way to get to medical care have a ride (a benefit called non-emergency medical transportation). People with both Medicare and full Medicaid generally get these rides from Medicaid, even when Medicare pays for the dialysis itself. People whose Medicaid only helps with Medicare premiums and cost-sharing (partial-benefit dual eligibility) do not. In MACPAC's analysis of 2018 Medicaid data, enrollees with kidney failure used these rides more often than any other diagnosis group it studied. (MACPAC, June 2021 report to Congress, chapter 5.)

    Medicare Advantage plans, which people with kidney failure can join, may cover some non-ambulance rides to dialysis as an extra benefit. It depends on the plan. (CMS booklet, section on transportation to dialysis facilities.)

    For other help with rides, Medicare's advice is to talk to the social worker at the dialysis center.

    How this was estimated

    Dialysis centers. Every Medicare-certified facility offering in-center hemodialysis in the CMS Dialysis Facility listing, located with the Census Bureau geocoder.

    Drive time. Times are floored at 2 minutes and ignore traffic, so real trips (especially in cities) usually take longer. Annual hours = one-way time × 2 × 3 sessions a week × 52 weeks.

    What routing changed.

    No road route.

    Households without a vehicle and the Social Vulnerability Index come from CDC/ATSDR SVI 2022 (American Community Survey 2018–2022). Without a car, the same trip by transit, paratransit or rides from others usually takes longer than the drive time shown, so those households' real time cost is understated here.

    Caveats. Estimates describe a typical resident of each neighborhood, not actual dialysis patients, who are older and more often Black or Hispanic than the population as a whole. Patients do not always use the nearest center (shift times, capacity, insurance and chain networks matter), and some dialyze at home instead. Connecticut county figures follow the 2020 Census counties. The map uses pre-2019 Alaska county shapes, so Chugach and Copper River are drawn together as the former Valdez-Cordova area; the ranking lists them separately.
