How far a Medicare Advantage network can send you

By Usnish Majumdar, MD · Updated November 2026

Medicare Advantage plans may limit members to a network of doctors and hospitals. CMS sets a maximum drive time to the nearest in-network provider of each specialty, county by county, and a plan passes if of the Medicare beneficiaries living in the county are within it, before the other credits described below.

    Alaska is mapped but not ranked: many communities have no road connection, and CMS limits there run to several hours.

    How this was estimated

    The rules. Maximum drive times by county and specialty come from the CMS HSD Reference File for plan year , which CMS uses to review Medicare Advantage networks under 42 CFR 422.116. Every county is one of five types (Large Metro, Metro, Micro, Rural, or Counties with Extreme Access Considerations) based on population and density, and each type has a base limit, looser the more rural the county. Where providers are too sparse for 90% of residents to reach one within the base limit, CMS raises the limit for that county (422.116(d)(3)); it never lowers it. There is also a maximum distance, not shown here. Under 422.116(d)(4), a network passes if at least of Medicare beneficiaries living in a large metro or metro county (CMS checks this against its own sample of where beneficiaries live, not the plan's enrollee list), and in a micro, rural or extreme-access county, live within the limits of at least one in-network provider. Under (d)(5), a plan that also contracts with telehealth providers gets a -point credit for a list of specialties that includes primary care, cardiology, nephrology, neurology, psychiatry and endocrinology, but not oncology, pulmonology, hospitals or nursing facilities. So in a rural county the telehealth credit alone lets a plan leave of beneficiaries beyond the cardiology limit and still pass. Plans in states with certificate-of-need laws can get a further credit, new applicants get one while CMS reviews them, and plans can request exceptions; none of those are counted here. The map compares the drive-time limit with how far older residents actually live from the nearest provider who treats Medicare patients; the gap is how much farther a plan's network can send them and still pass.

    Nearest provider. Clinicians come from the CMS Doctors and Clinicians national file (everyone enrolled in Medicare, by primary specialty; downloaded ), placed at the center of each practice ZIP code they are listed at. Counts on this page are clinicians (unique NPIs), not practice listings: cardiologists have clinician-and-ZIP-code listings across ZIP codes. Many specialists are also listed at small hospitals far from their main practice, where they hold a visiting clinic a few days a month. A ZIP code is left out of the distances when every clinician of that specialty listed there is also listed at a bigger practice more than km away. For cardiology that drops ZIP codes; counting them, the average drive for older rural residents to the nearest cardiologist would be minutes instead of . Primary care is internal medicine, family practice, general practice and geriatrics physicians; nurse practitioners and physician assistants are not counted. Oncology is medical, hematology/oncology and surgical oncology. Hospitals are Medicare-certified acute care and critical access hospitals, located with the Census geocoder; skilled nursing is every Medicare-certified nursing home (Medicaid-only facilities are left out), at CMS's coordinates.

    Drive time. For each census tract, the straight-line distance from its 2020 population center to the nearest provider was converted to driving minutes with a model fitted to real routes: Times are capped at minutes. County figures are averaged over residents 65 and older (CDC/ATSDR SVI 2022, from the 2018–2022 American Community Survey).

    Caveats. This compares the rules with the nearest provider of any kind, not with any plan's actual network, which can only be the same distance or farther. A provider listed in a ZIP code may not see new patients or accept a given plan. In small towns the nearest listed site is often a part-time clinic staffed by a group based elsewhere, even after visiting-only sites are dropped; several of the counties at the top of the "extra drive allowed" ranking are like this. ZIP-code placement adds error, especially in large rural ZIP codes whose center can be far from the town. The drive model is rough for short city trips and for long rural ones, where it runs long on interstates and short across mountains. The "past the limit" share depends on that model and is not shown for primary care, whose 10-to-40-minute limits are too short for it; it is also unreliable in large metro counties. CMS measures access with its own travel-time data. Alaska and Connecticut counties follow the codes in CMS enrollment files.
