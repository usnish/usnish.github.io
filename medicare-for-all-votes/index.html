Counting to 218: where every Democrat stands on Medicare for All

Democrats won both chambers on November 3, 2026, and Medicare for All polls better than it ever has. A bill still needs 218 House votes, 50 or 60 senators, and a president who will sign it. This page counts, member by member and with a source for each, who in the incoming 120th Congress is on record for it, and what it would take to get from there to a law.

    What 2027 to 2030 can and cannot do

    President Trump can veto any bill until noon on January 20, 2029. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each chamber, 290 House votes and 67 senators, more than the whole Democratic caucus in either, and the research found no Republican in Congress who supports single payer. So a Medicare for All bill cannot become law in 2027 or 2028. Those two years can produce hearings, a cost estimate from the Congressional Budget Office, recorded votes, new cosponsors, a Minnesota special election, the 2028 primaries, a party platform and a nominee.

    • Nov 18 and Dec 12, 2026Alaska's ranked-choice count decides whether the caucus is 55 or 56. Louisiana's 5th and 6th districts hold runoffs. Several close House races are still being counted.
    • Jan 3, 2027The 120th Congress is sworn in. Every bill from the 119th dies and has to be reintroduced, so the cosponsor count starts again from zero.
    • 2027Hearings are possible under committee leaders who are cosponsors: the research names Pallone at Energy and Commerce, McGovern at Rules, Boyle at Budget and Wyden at Senate Finance. Six of the seven likely House chairs of committees the bill is sent to are cosponsors. The exception is Neal at Ways and Means.
    • Aug 10 and Nov 2, 2027Minnesota's special primary and election for Amy Klobuchar's Senate seat (she was elected governor). The seat is filled by appointment until then.
    • Mar to Sept 2028Congressional and presidential primaries. The Democratic convention, and its platform, is Aug 7 to 10, 2028 (host city not yet chosen). In 2020 the platform committee voted down a Medicare for All amendment (KFF Health News).
    • Nov 7, 2028General election for the president, the whole House and a third of the Senate.
    • Jan 20, 2029The earliest date a president who backs Medicare for All could sign it. The American Rescue Plan, passed through budget reconciliation, was signed about 7 weeks into a term.
    • If signed in 2029Under the House bill, H.R.3069, everyone is covered 2 years after enactment, so 2031, with people under 19 and 55 and older covered a year earlier. Under the Senate bill, S.1506, benefits start January 1 of the fourth year after enactment, January 1, 2033, with children covered from 2030 and the Medicare age dropping to 55, 45 and then 35 on the way.

    Senate, in the order the research put them

    1. Minnesota's Klobuchar seat, by appointment and then the Nov 2, 2027 special.
    2. Senators who would need to change position: Ruben Gallego (cosponsored the House bill in three Congresses), Chris Murphy, Angela Alsobrooks, Andy Kim, Tammy Duckworth, Patty Murray and Maria Cantwell.
    3. 2028 primaries in New York (Schumer's seat) and Pennsylvania (Fetterman's seat).
    4. Republican-held seats in 2028 where a supporter could be the nominee: Florida and Wisconsin.
    5. Holding Ohio, Georgia, Arizona, Nevada and New Hampshire, and the six cosponsor seats up in 2028.
    6. Holding Vermont in 2030 if Sanders retires.

    Smaller bills could move first, but before 2029 only with the president's signature: Medicare dental, vision and hearing (H.R.2045, 132 cosponsors), a $5,000 cap on out-of-pocket costs in traditional Medicare (S.4886, 16), lowering the Medicare age to 50 (H.R.10875, 46), a Medicare buy-in at 50 to 64 (S.5700, 9), and waivers for state single-payer plans (H.R.4406, 35; S.2286, 3). In the states, Oregon's universal health plan board approved its draft report 9 to 0 on October 15, and its next steps, starting at its November 19 meeting, include a presentation to the legislature's health care committees (board presentation). New York's single-payer bill has majorities of both chambers as sponsors, 78 of 150 in the Assembly and 34 of 63 in the Senate (S3425), but has had no floor vote.

    For who the bills would cover (S.1506 section 102 makes residence, not work history, the test), see Who Medicare Leaves Uninsured at 65.

    Is there political will?

    For the goal, yes. For the mechanism, it is split. Among the people who would vote on it, not yet.

    For

    • KFF, September 2026: 66% favor and 34% oppose a national health plan, which the question says is sometimes called Medicare for All. That is the highest KFF has recorded. It was 53% in 2020 (KFF).
    • 64% (Gallup) and 66% (Pew) say the federal government should make sure everyone has coverage (Gallup, Pew).
    • Individual-market enrollment fell 18%, from 25.2 million to 20.5 million, after the enhanced subsidies expired, and insurers proposed a median 15% premium rise for 2027 (KFF).
    • Health care was the second most-picked issue in the midterms, behind the economy, and voters trusted Democrats on it 50 to 30 (KFF).
    • Four incoming senators ran on it. S.1506 has 18 cosponsors, against 14 in each of the three Congresses before (GovInfo).
    • Wyden, the senior Democrat on Senate Finance, cosponsored on September 24, 2026.

    Against

    • Support drops with specifics. Told it would eliminate private insurers: 52% favor, 47% oppose. Told it could raise taxes: 64% oppose. Told it could delay care: 76% oppose (KFF).
    • Asked about a plan “largely replacing private insurance”: 43% support, 24% oppose, 32% unsure (Axios-Ipsos, via Portside). Only 35% pick a single national government program (Pew).
    • Hakeem Jeffries, the Democratic leader, said in August 2026 that he does not cosponsor or support it (Newsweek). The leadership's health working group lists it only as an option to examine in the first year (memo).
    • Of the 2026 gains, 1 of 8 Senate seats and of about House seats went to supporters.
    • No committee has ever marked up a Medicare for All bill. The AMA and industry groups remain opposed.
    • The movement's own letter, signed by more than 300 organizations, names 2029 as the window (letter).

    About the data, and its limits

    Who is counted. Every Democrat and independent who caucuses with Democrats in the 120th Congress, as of , nine days after the election. In the House that is voting members, counting the Democrat leading each uncalled race; . In the Senate it is , 53 Democrats and 2 independents; Mary Peltola's Alaska race is uncalled and she is not counted (she backed a public option in 2022). Amy Klobuchar's row stands for whoever is appointed to her seat.

    Positions. “On record” means a sponsor or cosponsor of the 119th Congress bills (H.R.3069 had 118 cosponsors plus Jayapal; S.1506 had 18 plus Sanders; none withdrew) who returns in January, or a new member with a public statement of support. Everyone else was coded from their campaign or official site, interviews and news coverage into one of: public option, Medicare buy-in or a lower Medicare age, strengthen the ACA only, on record against Medicare for All, or no position found. Every row links to its source.

    Limits.

    • Unknowns are unknown. House members and senators have no position found, mostly because the search budget ran out. Some of them may support Medicare for All.
    • Results are not final. Mississippi's Senate race was called on November 11 by about half a point.
    • Soft supporters. count as supporters at medium or low confidence: a reporter's paraphrase, an automated interview transcript, a plan that never uses the name, or a race not yet called. Some supporters, such as Abdul El-Sayed in the Senate, back their own version, which he says would not have to ban extra coverage through unions or employers. Jasmine Clark (GA-13) is named as a supporter by The American Prospect but has no statement of her own here; counting her would make the House .
    • Old statements. Many non-supporter labels rest on statements from 2009 to 2020, including for Schumer, Reed, Rosen, Brown, Neal, McAdams, McBath, Bennet, Hickenlooper and Slotkin. Neal is coded from his on-the-record remarks in 2019; reports that he privately called Medicare for All a political loser came from anonymous accounts, so he is not coded as opposed. John Fetterman, who supported single payer in 2018 to 2020, is counted as no position found because nothing recent was found.
    • One coder. Each position was coded once by the research team and spot-checked for 32 rows, which found no invented quotes but four overstated labels; those were corrected. There is no second, independent coding.
    • Seat ratings use unofficial 2026 margins: competitive under 8 points, likely 8 to under 15, safe 15 or more. Several seats sit near 15 (CO-8, NY-19, CT-5, MI-3, NE-2). In California's Democrat-vs-Democrat races the margin is between two Democrats, so Doris Matsui's CA-7 reads as competitive though the seat is safe for the party; counting it as safe would put the all-safe-seats total at .
    • Projections are judgment, not a model. The 14% pickup rate is one election's: supporters among about House gains called by November 12 (NBC counts 43 flips, Fox 44). Counting the uncalled races Democrats lead, gains are closer to 47, and the rate about 13%. The Senate path research's best cases, about 32 to 37 supporters by January 2029 and 38 to 44 by 2031, are its judgment. That the Byrd rule would strike the core of the bill in reconciliation is the team's inference from the rule's text (CRS RL30862); no ruling or analysis specific to Medicare for All was found.
    • Corrections after fact-checking. A fact-check of the research on November 12 changed these before the page was built: Mississippi's Senate race moved from uncalled to won; Terri Sewell was added as a past cosponsor (2011); Richard Pan moved from opposed to ACA-only, Dan Koh from supporter to public option, and Fetterman to no position; El-Sayed and Katy Padilla Stout were lowered to medium confidence; CO-8 moved from safe to likely; several Senate margins were recomputed. A second check before publishing moved Richard Neal from opposed to ACA-only, because the only quote against Medicare for All was an anonymous account, and replaced two supporters' sources with their own words. The research's first counts, 118 House supporters and 213 with every safe seat, became and .
    • Sources. congress.gov, CBO, Axios, Cook and AP pages were often blocked, so the team used GovInfo bill-status files, the congress.gov API, reposts and Wikipedia instead. Several sources are advocates on one side or the other (Third Way, Data for Progress, the American Prospect, Common Dreams, PNHP).

    Method. A research team of agents built the roster on November 12, 2026: who won each seat, each member's position and source, and the path analysis.
