Counting to 218: the path to Medicare for All, 2027 to 2030

Democrats won both chambers on November 3, 2026, and Medicare for All polls better than it ever has. A bill still needs 218 House votes, 50 or 60 senators, and a president who will sign it. This page lays out what would have to happen, in order, from January 2027 through 2030 for it to become law, with the count at each step, and shows where every member of the incoming 120th Congress stands, with a source for each.

President Trump can veto any bill until noon on January 20, 2029. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each chamber, 290 House votes and 67 senators, more than the whole Democratic caucus in either, and the research found no Republican in Congress who supports single payer. So a Medicare for All bill cannot become law in 2027 or 2028. Those two years decide whether it can in 2029.

The path forward, 2027 to 2030

What would have to happen, in order, for Medicare for All to be signed into law, and who would have to do it. The bars under each stage show where the House and Senate counts would stand if every member named in that stage moved. They are ceilings, not forecasts. The difficulty rating on each stage is the author's judgment.

  1. Nov 18 to Dec 12, 2026

    Before the new Congress

    Alaska's ranked-choice count on November 18 decides whether the Senate caucus is 55 or 56. Senate Democrats are expected to elect their leader on December 1 (MS NOW); the majority leader decides what reaches the Senate floor. Louisiana's 5th and 6th districts hold runoffs on December 12, and several close House races are still being counted.

  2. Jan 3, 2027

    Reintroduce, and keep the base

    The 120th Congress is sworn in. Every bill from the 119th dies, so the House and Senate bills have to be reintroduced and the cosponsor lists start again from zero. The first test is whether all House members and senators counted here as on record sign on again.

    Who: the sponsors, Pramila Jayapal in the House and Bernie Sanders in the Senate, and every member already on record.

    Within reach
  3. 2027

    Hearings and a cost estimate

    H.R.3069 is sent to seven House committees (bill record). The senior Democrats on six of them, and so their likely chairs in a Democratic House, are cosponsors: Frank Pallone (Energy and Commerce), Jim McGovern (Rules), Bobby Scott (Education and Workforce), Jamie Raskin (Judiciary), Adam Smith (Armed Services) and Robert Garcia (Oversight) (committee leaders). The seventh is Ways and Means, which writes the financing; its senior Democrat, Richard Neal, is coded here as ACA only. Brendan Boyle, a cosponsor, is the senior Democrat on the Budget Committee. In the Senate the bill goes to Finance, where Ron Wyden, the senior Democrat, cosponsored on September 24, 2026.

    It has been done before. In 2019 the Rules (April 30), Budget (May 22) and Ways and Means (June 12) committees held hearings. No committee has ever marked up a Medicare for All bill. The research found no Congressional Budget Office estimate of either current bill; CBO's 2020 working paper describes how it would estimate one.

    Moves no votes. It produces a public record and a cost number that both sides will use.

    Within reach for hearings; a markup is harder
  4. 2027

    Grow the House list: the closest

    Two groups are closest. First, the Democrats who cosponsored an earlier Medicare for All bill but are not on the current one. They include the party's leader, Hakeem Jeffries, who said in August 2026 that he does not cosponsor or support it (Newsweek), and Whip Katherine Clark, Jim Clyburn, Joyce Beatty, Terri Sewell and Gwen Moore. Second, members in safe seats who are on record for a public option or a Medicare buy-in, among them Suzan DelBene (Ways and Means), Kim Schrier (Energy and Commerce) and Luke Bronin, a new member who replaced a cosponsor.

    Who: each of these members would need to change position. The research found no stated reason for any former cosponsor leaving the list.

    Hard
  5. 2027

    Grow the Senate list

    The Senate path research put seven senators first: Ruben Gallego, who cosponsored the House bill in three Congresses; Chris Murphy and Angela Alsobrooks, on record for a Medicare buy-in; Patty Murray, on record for a public option; Maria Cantwell, coded ACA only; and Andy Kim and Tammy Duckworth, with no position found. Each would need to change position.

    The Senate bill had 16 cosponsors in 2017 (S.1804), 14 in 2019 (S.1129), 14 in 2023 (S.1655) and has 18 now. Seven more in one Congress would be a larger jump than any so far.

    Hard
  6. Aug 10 and Nov 2, 2027

    Minnesota's special election

    Amy Klobuchar was elected governor. Her Senate seat is filled by appointment, by Klobuchar or by Governor Walz depending on when she resigns, and then by a special primary on August 10 and election on November 2, 2027, if she resigns before May 25 (KTTC). The winner serves through January 3, 2031. Minnesota elected Peggy Flanagan, a supporter, to its other seat in 2026.

    Who: the appointee, then Minnesota's primary and general election voters. Klobuchar's row on this page is coded public option and stands for whoever is appointed.

    Possible
  7. 2027 to 2028

    A recorded vote

    A floor vote puts every member on record. The Speaker decides what reaches the House floor, and Jeffries, the Democratic leader, does not support the bill. The other route is a discharge petition: once a bill has sat in committee for 30 legislative days, 218 signatures can force a vote (CRS). With on record, a petition would mostly work as a public whip count. Anything that passed both chambers before January 20, 2029 would face a veto.

    Moves no votes by itself. It shows who is where.

    Hard
  8. Jan to Sept 2028

    Primaries, and the rest of the safe seats

    Positions change in two ways: a member changes position, or a supporter wins the nomination. After the first two House groups, Democrats in safe seats remain: who back strengthening the ACA only, with no position found and on record against. The House count needs all of them. They include Neal and Steven Horsford (Ways and Means); Raul Ruiz, Lizzie Fletcher, Scott Peters and Jennifer McClellan (Energy and Commerce); and La Shawn Ford and Oliver Gilbert, new members who replaced cosponsors.

    In the Senate, the research named the 2028 primaries for Chuck Schumer's seat in New York and John Fetterman's in Pennsylvania. Schumer is on record for a public option. Fetterman supported single payer from 2018 to 2020; nothing recent was found. Pennsylvania is also a state Trump won, so a nominee would then have to win in November.

    Presidential primaries run from January to June on FHQ's tentative calendar, with California and Texas on March 7. Congressional primaries run through September.

  9. Aug 7 to 10, 2028

    The platform and the nominee

    The Democratic convention adopts a platform and nominates a president (dates; host city not yet chosen). In 2020 the platform committee voted down a Medicare for All amendment (KFF Health News). At an October 2026 forum in Las Vegas, one of four possible candidates, Ro Khanna, said he wants to tax billionaires to help pay for Medicare for All; the other three did not directly answer (Nevada Independent).

    Moves no votes on its own, but it is the stage that could move the most. Sixty Democrats voted for the ACA, and most had never cosponsored anything like it.

    Hard
  10. Nov 7, 2028

    The general election

    House. Hold the supporters in close seats: . Win seats where the 2026 Democratic nominee ran on Medicare for All: TN-9 and ME-2, which Republicans won in 2026, and CA-23 if the trailing Democrat loses this year's count. Keep the majority. On top of every earlier stage, that brings the House to exactly 218, with no room to lose a single seat. The non-supporters in likely seats are the only cushion.

    Senate. Democratic seats are up. are held by cosponsors who would need to be held: . Democrats also defend five states Trump won in 2024: Arizona, Nevada, Pennsylvania, Georgia and Ohio (2028 map). Republicans defend 18 seats. The research named two where a supporter could be the Democratic nominee: Florida, where Ashley Moody won the 2026 special election by a closer-than-expected margin, and Wisconsin, where Ron Johnson has indicated he will not run again.

    President. A nominee who would sign the bill has to win.

    Best realistic case, as judgment: the Senate path research put it at about 32 to 37 senators by January 2029; the named moves above add up to . The research made no House forecast. The House figure here is a ceiling that needs every safe-seat Democrat.

    Hard
  11. Jan 3 and Jan 20, 2029

    The window opens

    The 121st Congress is sworn in and the new president is inaugurated, the earliest date a president who backs Medicare for All could sign it. From here a bill needs 218 House votes, and in the Senate either 60 votes or 50 plus a Democratic vice president. Sixty would take every Democrat plus Republicans, and none was found who supports single payer.

    Fifty works two ways. Budget reconciliation avoids the filibuster, but the Byrd rule strikes provisions whose budget effect is “merely incidental” and takes 60 votes to waive (CRS RL30862). The research team's inference, not a ruling, is that this would strike the core of the bill, such as the ban on duplicate private insurance and the rules for providers. The other way is changing the filibuster rule, which itself takes 50 votes. John Fetterman wrote in July 2026 that his earlier support for ending the filibuster was “horribly wrong” (Wikipedia); James Talarico said in February 2026 that he supports eliminating the current filibuster (Texas Tribune). The research found no full count.

    On the named moves the Senate stands at , short of 50. Those would have to come from the remaining non-supporters, of them in states Trump won in 2024 or seats Republicans held before 2026. For speed, the American Rescue Plan, passed through reconciliation, was signed about 7 weeks into a term.

    Very hard: not on current numbers
  12. 2029

    The step the numbers support

    A smaller bill has more senators behind it. of the are on record for Medicare for All, a public option or a Medicare buy-in, short of 50. The rest would come from the other : coded ACA only, with no position found and on record against. Bills for this are already filed: lowering the Medicare age to 50 (H.R.10875, 46 cosponsors) and a Medicare buy-in at 50 to 64 (S.5700, 9). As an inference, money pieces like these fit reconciliation better than the full bill.

    This order has been proposed before. Elizabeth Warren's 2019 plan used reconciliation in the first 100 days for a Medicare for All option and a Medicare choice from age 50, then aimed to pass full Medicare for All no later than the third year (plan). S.1506 itself lowers the Medicare age to 55, 45 and then 35 before full benefits start.

    Other bills could move alongside, but before 2029 only with the president's signature: Medicare dental, vision and hearing (H.R.2045, 132 cosponsors), a $5,000 cap on out-of-pocket costs in traditional Medicare (S.4886, 16), and waivers for state single-payer plans (H.R.4406, 35; S.2286, 3).

    Hard, but the closest to the numbers
  13. 2030 and after

    Phase-in, and the next Senate map

    If a bill is signed in 2029: under the House bill, H.R.3069, everyone is covered 2 years after enactment, so 2031, with people under 19 and 55 and older covered a year earlier. Under the Senate bill, S.1506, benefits start January 1 of the fourth year after enactment, January 1, 2033, with children covered from 2030 and the Medicare age dropping to 55, 45 and then 35 on the way.

    In November 2030, Democratic-caucus seats are up, of them held by supporters, including Bernie Sanders, who has said this term will likely be his last. Gallego, Murphy, Alsobrooks, Kim, Cantwell and the Minnesota seat are also up. Democrats defend four states Trump won in 2024 (Arizona, Michigan, Nevada and Wisconsin); Republicans defend Mississippi, Ohio and Texas, which elected Democratic senators in 2026, and Pennsylvania (2030 map). It is also the new president's first midterm.

    The Senate path research's best case for January 2031 is about 38 to 44 supporters. That is its judgment, and still short of 50.

Outside Congress: Oregon's universal health plan board approved its draft report 9 to 0 on October 15, and its next steps, starting at its November 19 meeting, include a presentation to the legislature's health care committees (board presentation). New York's single-payer bill has majorities of both chambers as sponsors, 78 of 150 in the Assembly and 34 of 63 in the Senate (S3425), but has had no floor vote.

What has to be true

  1. A president who would sign it, in office from January 20, 2029. Before then a veto stands unless 290 House members and 67 senators override it.
  2. 218 House votes, more than today. Counting every former cosponsor, every safe-seat Democrat and three pickups, the count reaches exactly 218.
  3. 50 senators and a Democratic vice president, with the filibuster changed, or 60 senators. Today it is ; the named moves through 2028 reach .
  4. Democratic majorities in both chambers after November 2028.
  5. Leaders who bring it to the floor: the Speaker and the Senate majority leader.

Which condition binds. On these numbers, the Senate's 50th vote, and behind it the 2028 nominee. The House can reach 218 on paper by moving members in safe seats, where pressure and primaries can reach. The Senate has no such reserve. If every non-supporter outside states Trump won, or seats Republicans held before 2026, came over, the count would be . The rest would have to come from senators in those states, and the Senate list has stayed between 14 and 18 since 2017. Votes have moved like that before: 60 Democrats voted for the ACA, its president's signature bill, and most had never cosponsored anything like it. So in practice the condition that decides the others is a 2028 nominee who runs on Medicare for All and wins. As one health policy scholar put it after the election, “just because Democrats have a majority doesn’t mean it’s a majority for Medicare for All” (KFF Health News). Without such a nominee, the senators behind a Medicare option make that the likelier 2029 law. This is judgment, not a model.

    Is there political will?

    For the goal, yes. For the mechanism, it is split. Among the people who would vote on it, not yet.

    For

    • KFF, September 2026: 66% favor and 34% oppose a national health plan, which the question says is sometimes called Medicare for All. That is the highest KFF has recorded. It was 53% in 2020 (KFF).
    • 64% (Gallup) and 66% (Pew) say the federal government should make sure everyone has coverage (Gallup, Pew).
    • Individual-market enrollment fell 18%, from 25.2 million to 20.5 million, after the enhanced subsidies expired, and insurers proposed a median 15% premium rise for 2027 (KFF).
    • Health care was the second most-picked issue in the midterms, behind the economy, and voters trusted Democrats on it 50 to 30 (KFF).
    • Four incoming senators ran on it. S.1506 has 18 cosponsors, against 16 in 2017 and 14 in 2019 and 2023 (GovInfo).
    • Wyden, the senior Democrat on Senate Finance, cosponsored on September 24, 2026.

    Against

    • Support drops with specifics. Told it would eliminate private insurers: 52% favor, 47% oppose. Told it could raise taxes: 64% oppose. Told it could delay care: 76% oppose (KFF).
    • Asked about a plan “largely replacing private insurance”: 43% support, 24% oppose, 32% unsure (Axios-Ipsos, via Portside). Only 35% pick a single national government program (Pew).
    • Hakeem Jeffries, the Democratic leader, said in August 2026 that he does not cosponsor or support it (Newsweek). The leadership's health working group lists it only as an option to examine in the first year (memo).
    • Of the 2026 gains, 1 of 8 Senate seats and of about House seats went to supporters.
    • No committee has ever marked up a Medicare for All bill. The AMA and industry groups remain opposed.
    • The movement's own letter, signed by more than 300 organizations, names 2029 as the window (letter).

    About the data, and its limits

    Who is counted. Every Democrat and independent who caucuses with Democrats in the 120th Congress, as of , nine days after the election. In the House that is voting members, counting the Democrat leading each uncalled race; . In the Senate it is , 53 Democrats and 2 independents; Mary Peltola's Alaska race is uncalled and she is not counted (she backed a public option in 2022). Amy Klobuchar's row stands for whoever is appointed to her seat.

    Positions. “On record” means a sponsor or cosponsor of the 119th Congress bills (H.R.3069 had 118 cosponsors plus Jayapal; S.1506 had 18 plus Sanders; none withdrew) who returns in January, or a new member with a public statement of support. Everyone else was coded from their campaign or official site, interviews and news coverage into one of: public option, Medicare buy-in or a lower Medicare age, strengthen the ACA only, on record against Medicare for All, or no position found. Every row links to its source.

    Limits.

    • Unknowns are unknown. House members and senators have no position found, mostly because the search budget ran out. Some of them may support Medicare for All.
    • Results are not final. Mississippi's Senate race was called on November 11 by about half a point.
    • Soft supporters. count as supporters at medium or low confidence: a reporter's paraphrase, an automated interview transcript, a plan that never uses the name, or a race not yet called. Some supporters, such as Abdul El-Sayed in the Senate, back their own version, which he says would not have to ban extra coverage through unions or employers. Jasmine Clark (GA-13) is named as a supporter by The American Prospect but has no statement of her own here; counting her would make the House .
    • Old statements. Many non-supporter labels rest on statements from 2009 to 2020, including for Schumer, Reed, Rosen, Brown, Neal, McAdams, McBath, Bennet, Hickenlooper and Slotkin. Neal is coded from his on-the-record remarks in 2019; reports that he privately called Medicare for All a political loser came from anonymous accounts, so he is not coded as opposed. John Fetterman, who supported single payer in 2018 to 2020, is counted as no position found because nothing recent was found.
    • One coder. Each position was coded once by the research team and spot-checked for 32 rows, which found no invented quotes but four overstated labels; those were corrected. There is no second, independent coding.
    • Seat ratings use unofficial 2026 margins: competitive under 8 points, likely 8 to under 15, safe 15 or more. Several seats sit near 15 (CO-8, NY-19, CT-5, MI-3, NE-2). In California's Democrat-vs-Democrat races the margin is between two Democrats, so Doris Matsui's CA-7 reads as competitive though the seat is safe for the party; counting it as safe would put the all-safe-seats total at .
    • The path's counts are ceilings. Each running count assumes every member named in a stage moves, and the groups and seats named come from the research team's path analysis. The difficulty ratings are the author's judgment. No stage is a forecast.
    • Projections are judgment, not a model. The 14% pickup rate is one election's: supporters among about House gains called by November 12 (NBC counts 43 flips, Fox 44). Counting the uncalled races Democrats lead, gains are closer to 47, and the rate about 13%. The Senate path research's best cases, about 32 to 37 supporters by January 2029 and 38 to 44 by 2031, are its judgment. That the Byrd rule would strike the core of the bill in reconciliation is the team's inference from the rule's text (CRS RL30862); no ruling or analysis specific to Medicare for All was found.
    • Corrections after fact-checking. A fact-check of the research on November 12 changed these before the page was built: Mississippi's Senate race moved from uncalled to won; Terri Sewell was added as a past cosponsor (2011); Richard Pan moved from opposed to ACA-only, Dan Koh from supporter to public option, and Fetterman to no position; El-Sayed and Katy Padilla Stout were lowered to medium confidence; CO-8 moved from safe to likely; several Senate margins were recomputed.
