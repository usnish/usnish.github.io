Two federal standards that disagree about the same homes

Census Bureau statistical definition

A “limited English speaking household” is one in which “no member 14 years old and over (1) speaks only English at home or (2) speaks a language other than English at home and speaks English ‘very well.’” “All members of a household are included in this group, including members under 14 years old who may speak only English or speak a language other than English at home and speak English ‘very well.’” The variable “identifies households that may need English-language assistance.” 2024 ACS Subject Definitions, p. 55. KIDS COUNT’s indicator of children in linguistically isolated households is built on it.

Federal civil-rights rule for health care

A covered entity must not “rely on a minor child to interpret or facilitate communication, except as a temporary measure while finding a qualified interpreter in an emergency involving an imminent threat to the safety or welfare of an individual or the public where there is no qualified interpreter … immediately available.” 45 CFR 92.201(e)(3), Section 1557 rule, text as shown on eCFR (current as of November 6, 2026; this version in effect since July 5, 2024; a similar bar has been in the rule since 2016).

About the data

Source. U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey 2020–2024 5-year Public Use Microdata Sample (person and housing files; data dictionary). People in group quarters (dorms, nursing homes, prisons) are left out.

Definitions. A child is fluent if the household’s answer says they speak only English at home, or speak another language and speak English “very well.” An adult (18 or older) has limited English under the standard definition if they speak another language at home and speak English “well,” “not well” or “not at all” (less than “very well,” the usual federal data definition), and under the strict definition if “not well” or “not at all.” A bridge child is a fluent child aged 5 to 17 in a home where every adult has limited English.

Method. Person counts use the person weight; household counts and the “out of 10 homes” rate use the housing-unit weight. Standard errors come from the 80 replicate weights with the Census Bureau’s successive-difference formula, SE = √(4/80 × Σ(replicate − estimate)²); ranges shown are 90% margins of error (1.645 × SE). The Census limited-English household flag was recomputed from the person records and Household language is the language of the householder (the ACS reference person, usually the person in whose name the home is owned or rented), or of the first adult listed if the householder is under 18. Language rates are shown only where at least 100 sample homes have a school-age child; state counts are hidden when the standard error is more than 30% of the estimate.

What this does and does not show. One person answers the survey for everyone in the home, so a child’s English rating is a parent’s report, and “very well” for a 6-year-old means fluent for their age, not able to explain a medication change. Being the only fluent speaker at home does not prove a child interprets, and relatives or neighbors outside the home are not seen. Studies of youth language brokering find it consequential: a 2022 meta-analysis of 65 studies (Shen et al., American Psychologist; PubMed 36190758) linked more frequent brokering with somewhat worse family relationships and more acculturation stress, with how children felt about it mattering more than how often they did it. Many adults counted here speak English “well” ( under the standard definition), which is why the strict count is also shown.

The Census measure is not wrong. Its 14-and-over rule is a deliberate statistical convention, used the same way since the ACS began, and it answers a different question: is there someone at home who can handle English? Health-care civil-rights rules answer that question differently for anyone under 18. This page puts the two side by side; it does not find an error in either.

Prior work. A 2019 Census Bureau poster by Sandy Dietrich and Kurt Bauman (SEHSD-WP2019-16, 2017 ACS) saw this group: 20.7% of English-fluent children aged 5–17 in homes where someone speaks another language lived where every adult spoke English less than very well. The poster studied what predicts children’s English and did not count the children, profile their ages or households, or connect them to health-care rules. KFF’s September 2026 overview of people with limited English proficiency counts 28.5 million people aged 5 and older with limited English (2024 ACS), by language and state; it does not count households, the children in them, or mention the rule on minors. A figure of 11 million child language brokers is widely repeated but has no traceable source; it is not comparable to the count here.
