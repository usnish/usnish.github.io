The 65th birthday that never comes: who Medicare leaves uninsured

By Usnish Majumdar, MD · November 2026

Medicare is the closest thing the United States has to single-payer health care, and at 65 nearly everyone born here gets it. But Medicare is earned, either with about 10 years of US payroll taxes or (for a green-card holder) by living here 5 years and then paying a monthly premium. People who move here late in life often have neither.

The price of buying in, and who would pay it

Earned, or by residence

Medicare for All bills would replace the earning rules with residence. Meanwhile, the 2025 budget law moved Medicare the other way for some immigrants.

Medicare today

Earned. Premium-free Part A comes with 40 quarters (about 10 years) of work under Social Security, your own or a spouse's.

Bought. Without that record, a US citizen, or a permanent resident who has lived here 5 years in a row, can buy Part A plus Part B .

Without the work record, no one else 65 or older can enroll, however long they have lived here.

Until then. Lawfully present older immigrants can buy a Marketplace plan, with premium subsidies through 2026; from 2027 only permanent residents, Cuban and Haitian entrants and COFA migrants keep the subsidies. Most permanent residents also wait 5 years for Medicaid (Justice in Aging).

The 2025 budget law (Pub. L. 119-21, sec. 71201)

Limits Medicare to citizens, permanent residents, certain Cuban and Haitian entrants and people living here under the Compacts of Free Association.

Refugees, people granted asylum, people with Temporary Protected Status and some other lawfully present immigrants lose eligibility. Those already enrolled lose coverage by January 4, 2027, according to KFF and Justice in Aging.

Medicare for All Act (S.1506 / H.R.3069, 2025), sec. 102

“Every individual who is a resident of the United States is entitled to benefits…” The bills leave the test of residency to a rule from the Secretary of Health and Human Services, and let the Secretary cover others.

Both bills also bar travel to the US “for the sole purpose of obtaining health care items and services” (the Senate bill by a rule from the Secretary). The same section 102 appeared in the 2023 bills, S.1655 and H.R.3421.

Nothing on this page says which of the uninsured could enroll under any of these rules, because the survey does not record immigration status (see below).

What could change this

Congress could end the two five-year waits. A permanent resident 65 or older without enough work history must live here 5 years in a row before buying into Medicare (42 USC 1395i-2(a) and 1395o(a)), and most wait 5 years for Medicaid, the program that pays Medicare premiums for people with low incomes (Justice in Aging). The HEAL for Immigrant Families Act of 2025 (H.R. 4104 / S. 2149) would replace both waits with lawful presence. About 72,000 uninsured people 65 and older arrived less than 5 years ago, one in four of all people that age who arrived that recently. Most of the work would fall to the Medicaid half, since 82.1% of uninsured people 65 and older who arrived less than 10 years ago have personal income below a year of the full buy-in ($9,215 in 2026). Not all 72,000 would gain, since the survey cannot tell permanent residents from long-stay visitors or people without status, and the uninsured rate falls more between years 1 and 2 (28.2% to 20.9%), where no rule changes, than at year 5 (19.8% to 14.5%). The objection is the cost of covering people who have paid little into Medicare, and the 2025 law moved the other way.

States can act without Congress. Seven states (counting DC) use their own money to cover some adults whatever their immigration status, though several have closed enrollment. New York's covers only people 65 and older, and Illinois's has taken no new enrollees since 2023 (KFF). Under the Medicare for All bills above, coverage for this group would turn on the residency test, which the bills leave to a later rule; Congress could write it, and any waiting period, into the bill. Canada caps a province's wait for new residents at three months as a condition of federal health funding (Canada Health Act, s. 11).

About the data

Source. US Census Bureau, American Community Survey (ACS) Public Use Microdata Sample person files: the 2024 1-year file (who the uninsured are), the 2022, 2023 and 2024 1-year files pooled (age curves, years in the US, recent arrivals), and the 2020–2024 5-year file (age at arrival and language at 65 and older). Downloaded from census.gov. All figures cover the civilian noninstitutional population, the universe ACS uses for health insurance; people in nursing homes, prisons and other institutions are left out.

Method. Uninsured means no health insurance of any kind at the time of the survey (HICOV). Age at arrival is the age now minus the years since the year of entry (YOEP), so it can be off by a year. All rates are weighted. 95% confidence intervals use the Census Bureau's 80 replicate weights (successive difference replication). For the pooled years every weight is divided by three. Single-year-of-age points with fewer than 100 survey records are not shown. Language rows need at least 100 records. Limited English means speaking English less than “very well”. Grouped categories such as “Other languages of Asia” are left out of the language chart.

Caveats.

  • The ACS does not ask about immigration status. “Not a citizen” mixes permanent residents, refugees and people granted asylum, people with Temporary Protected Status or other temporary status, and people without status. So the data cannot say who could buy into Medicare. The rules on this page sit beside the data and are not used to classify anyone in it.
  • The ACS counts people staying at an address for more than two months, so some recent arrivals, especially in their first year or two, are long-stay visitors (such as parents visiting on tourist visas) rather than immigrants.
  • The year of entry is the most recent arrival, so people who lived here before, left and came back look like recent arrivals.
  • The missing drop at 65 for people who arrive at 60 or older is no surprise or malfunction; it is what the work-quarter and residency rules produce. This page is about how many people it touches and who they are.
  • Coverage is self-reported. People whose only care is through the Indian Health Service are counted as uninsured, as the ACS does.
  • The 2020–2024 file spans the pandemic years, when Medicaid kept people enrolled continuously.
  • Language rows from small groups have wide intervals. The West and East African languages are shown to set them against the rest, not to rank them against each other.

Prior work. Other researchers have shown that Medicare's coverage jump at 65 is smaller for immigrants as a whole: Park and colleagues, using a regression discontinuity in the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (J Am Geriatr Soc 2023), and Jun and colleagues in the Health and Retirement Study (The Gerontologist 2024). The Congressional Research Service reported in 2009 that Medicare reached 69% of noncitizens 65 and older, against 94% of the US-born (CRS R40772). KFF's Key Facts on Health Coverage of Immigrants gives uninsured rates for adults by immigration status. Yin and colleagues (Generations, 2025, ACS 2019–23) showed Medicare participation by age lags for people who arrive at 50 or older, as O'Neil and Tienda found earlier (J Gerontol B 2015). This page adds the uninsured rate, rather than Medicare receipt, by single year of age and age at arrival, the make-up of the uninsured 65 and older by nativity, citizenship, arrival age and English ability, rates by years in the US, and rates by detailed language.
